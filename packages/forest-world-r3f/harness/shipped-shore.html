The landform that falls to the shore — three widths, and two inset rings

The shipped 3D ground now ends on a real coastline, but the land does not fall to it. landRelief is an unbounded sum of three sine waves and a function of position only — there is no shore term anywhere in it — so the beach the coast clip added on 2026-09-01 stands at whatever height the sine sum happens to give it, and an island can finish at a vertical cut several units above the waterline. The approved treatment’s second component is “a landform that falls to the shore”, and its generator’s own comment says why: an island whose ground is flat right up to a vertical cut has no shore, and a shore is most of what makes a coast read.

The numbers here are the approved render’s own, not taste. docs/research/chapter2-land-idiom-2026-08-27/build_land.py is the script that produced the picture the owner stamped. Its landform is four lines: a shore band BEACH = 3.1 ground units wide, a smoothstep across it, the relief multiplied by that, and the beach dipped 0.62 units below the grass line. The two scales agree — the generator sizes its island from “the real island’s ground footprint”, 233.8 units, and the shipped island measures 234 — so both constants transfer as authored rather than being re-tuned.

THE WIDTH AXIS — how far inland the fall reaches
none      the map today — full height right up to the waterline (CONTROL)
authored  a 3.1-unit band — the generator’s own number, under half our beach
beach     a 7-unit band — exactly the land the coast clip added
shelf     a 16.5-unit band — one whole parcel inland, a shelf not a lip

THE RING AXIS — the SAME 7-unit band, with vertices inside it to bend through
ring      + one inset ring at 3.5 units, the band’s midpoint
ring-pair + two inset rings at the band’s thirds

⚠⚠ The width turned out not to be a knob, and that is why the ring axis exists. On the shipped island 253 of 392 distinct ground vertices lie exactly on the coast, the nearest interior vertex is 8.66 ground units away, and there is nothing in between. Every band narrower than that void acts on the rim vertices alone, so authored (3.1) and beach (7) deliver the bit-identical land — same moved count, same drops, same rung flips, and the two committed PNGs are the same file. The cause is structural: the approved render’s generator displaces a 0.55-unit grid, while this ground is parcels ~16.5 units across whose only vertices are their corners. The mesh is about thirty times coarser than the surface every transcribed constant was authored on. The remedy for a geometry-valued component is not a different constant — it is more vertices.

⚠⚠ So the ring arms move the MESH and hold the band fixed. They divide each coastal parcel’s top face along a chain of vertices at a fixed shore distance, so the falloff has something to bend through. beach is their control: same 7-unit band, same dip, same everything, one mesh apart. It costs triangles, which the fall itself did not, so this page carries a cost table it did not need before — and the honest outcome a refusal is written for is that a ring which cost triangles and delivered no visible change is a refinement to decline.

⚠ The wall follows the divided outline too, and that is the half a reader would not think to check. A top face that bends through a point on an edge, over a wall that spans that edge straight, is a hairline crack running the length of the shore. And where two parcels share an edge, both must insert the same point: the rule is edge-local — a point goes on an edge exactly when the inset lies between its two endpoints’ shore distances, bisected in the edge’s canonical orientation — so two parcels computing it independently get the identical float.

⚠ A coast can turn tighter than its own ring, and the cap says so. An inward offset of a curve self-intersects as soon as it exceeds the curve’s radius of curvature, and this coast is a noise-perturbed Chaikin curve whose headlands sometimes do. The chain is then demoted down the coast clip’s own scale ladder until it holds, keeping a shallower band rather than none — and the captions report how many parcels were capped and how far, because a cap nobody can see is indistinguishable from a shore that never needed one.

⚠⚠ Why three arms: the fork is a number mismatch, not a design choice. The reference’s shore band is 3.1 units wide. The beach this map draws is COAST_OUTSET = 7 units, more than twice that. So transcribing the authored number faithfully leaves over half of our own beach standing at full height, and the honest thing is to show all three and let the picture decide. Only the width moves. Every arm is clipped to the same shipped coast, carries the same 0.62 dip, and wears the same ladder, grain, occlusion atlas, light and camera.

⚠⚠ The geometry counters are identical across the WIDTH arms, and that is the result rather than an oversight. The fall moves vertices in Y and creates none, so it cannot move a triangle, a ring vertex, an attribute byte or a square unit of land — the component is exactly free in every count the coast increment spent. A triangle delta on a width arm would be a bug, and the driver refuses the run. The ring arms are the mirror image: they must spend triangles, and they must not move one square unit of land. Ground lost is a hole in the island; ground drawn twice is one capability’s status colour painted over another’s, which is the one way this component could do real harm. Conserved area is the evidence that a divided parcel is still the same parcel.

⚠⚠ And the sag is the number this increment exists to move. shoreRelief is analytic — it answers the smoothstep at every point — but what the map draws is a triangulation that samples it at the vertices and interpolates flat between them. With no vertex inside the band, the drawn shore is a straight ramp from the waterline to the first corner 8.66 units inland, and the falloff’s shape is not coarse but absent. The sag is that gap, measured per band triangle between its own plane and the field at its centroid. It is the honest form of “did the shape become visible”, because it is a property of the surface rather than of the pictures — and the pixel columns beside it are what say a viewer would actually see the difference.

⚠ The rung flips are the only number a viewer can actually see. The banded material quantises dot(n, L) onto the authored ladder, so a moved normal is a moved rung is a different delivered colour. A band that moved a lot of geometry but flipped no rung would be invisible on the shipped material however deep its drop, and a geometry figure alone cannot tell those two apart. The count is a lower bound: it is taken per vertex, while the shader quantises per fragment, so rung boundaries also fall between vertices where this cannot see them.

⚠ The normal carries the product rule, which is this component’s whole risk. Scaling the height by a falloff without differentiating the falloff too gives a surface lit for the shape it had before the shore was cut — and on a banded material a slightly-wrong normal is not a slightly-wrong colour, it is a different rung. That mistake is invisible in the bounds, the silhouette and the picture. The suite holds the analytic normal to a central difference of the height the same object returns.

⚠ Read the per-object number, not the frame percentage. A shore band is a thin annulus, so “0.4% of the frame changed” reads as nothing beside two pictures that are obviously different lands. Here the control and the reference are the same arm, so an arm-vs-control figure already is the band’s own footprint; the denominator earns its keep on the arm-vs-arm table, where 3.1 against 7 against 16.5 is the actual question.

⚠ And the band’s delivered width in pixels is printed beside every comparison. At the fitted zoom a 7-unit band is under three pixels, so an arm that changed the whole landform can come back nearly byte-identical there. A null result has to be readable as a null result rather than as agreement.
